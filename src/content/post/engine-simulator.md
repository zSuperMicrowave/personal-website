# Engine Sound Simulator

## Introduction

For years now, I've been obsessed with how car engines sound. I love their dynamics, their energy, and their complexity.\
Engine sounds aren't simple, videogames often try to imitate them, but they do so by **sampling** the sound of real engines at different RPMs and then piecing them together by altering the pitch and volume of different engine loops.

At first glance, this might seem like a perfect replica of the original sound. However, this method has significant limitations: as soon as the RPM changes by even a small amount, the sound alters through a pitch shift rather than being generated by the pistons firing faster. This makes the sound feel less realistic as you shift RPM, increase engine load, or change how air flows into the cylinders.\
Every detail makes a difference.

So, what's the most rational, realistic, and feasible solution?\
Of course, it's to simulate everything from scratch!

I may have underestimated the task, but the end result is incredibly cool!\
[Check it out here!](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7ZMjbiQeR78)

## How It Works

### Foundamentals

The basic idea is to simulate pressure waves inside the engine's cylinders to produce a dynamic sound.
This is very similar to how Ange's first simulator works, except that I add an important step between sound generation and exhaust reverberation: the reverberation inside each cylinder.

It is my hypothesis, and so far, it seems to hold true, that one of the reasons engines have unique sounds is the constant bouncing of noise within what isn’t just a simple cylinder with constant dimensions. Instead, it acts as a reverberation chamber, with volume and pressure constantly changing. This makes a big difference because sound accelerates as pressure increases and reflects more as volume decreases.

This effect is particularly noticeable in low-speed engines that don’t have an exhaust manifold. In these engines, it’s possible to clearly hear a pulsating noise that rises in pitch before stopping, much like a bubbling sound.
My hypothesis is that the noise we hear in such cases is caused by the chamber's changing size.

### Exhaust

Next comes the exhaust part, which consists of a series of delay filters for each cylinder that then merge into additional delay filters.
I’ve been using this method for a long time, and it seemed to work well enough. However, one critical aspect I had been missing for a long time was sound bouncing back to previous delay filters.

The issue was that each filter used to work independently. I had been aware of this problem for a long time, but it was so difficult to fix that I kept ignoring it—until one day, I came across a video explaining some interesting facts about sound in engine exhaust systems. It introduced me to an incredibly cool and simple approximation of real life.

The basic principle is that when multiple tubes merge together, sound reflects back, causing noise to travel through all the tubes—not just the one that generated the sound.
A more in-depth explanation can be found at this link, around the 7:00 mark of the video: [Hey, click here!](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BSUHB9AB_6Y)

## Project Objective

Some of you might know AngeTheGreat on YouTube and think I just stole his idea and made it worse.
While it’s true that I’ve drawn inspiration from some of his ideas, I like to see my simulator as a project developed with very specific goals in mind. Unlike Ange's simulator, which is grounded in a physics-based approach that offers total flexibility, mine is based on mathematical modeling tailored specifically to engine behavior.

This approach theoretically allows for a more optimized simulation in certain scenarios and can sometimes make development faster. However, more often than not, it presents unique challenges. To create something functional, I need to design it with clear and specific objectives from the outset.

For instance, I don’t have a highly generic physics simulation where I can simply ask, "Hey, could you provide me with the velocity of that object?" Instead, I have to build the entire system with the specific requirement of accessing that object’s velocity in mind. At first glance, this might seem obvious and even harmless, but what happens if I want to add a destruction system? Ange could simply query his simulation for collisions, velocities, and other parameters. On the other hand, I’d need to redesign significant parts of my system to accommodate destructibility.

So why am I taking this approach? Why am I building something that feels prone to breaking?
First, because it’s extremely cool and people here coming from my CV will hopefully appreciate this... Please 🥺 👉👈
Second, because I genuinely believe in the power of modularity.

My first attempt has been a disaster, and that’s perfectly fine. Prototypes are meant to break, they're made to test functionality. However, I’m now in the process of rewriting everything in C++ with clear objectives in mind. This new system is being designed to leave room for additional functionality, such as non-piston engines, unconventional layouts, opposed-piston engines, forced induction systems, engine failure, and modular components, among others.

The rewritten code is structured to be as modular as possible. While the mechanical processing loop operates hierarchically, the fluid simulation system runs independently, allowing for greater flexibility. For example, multiple pistons can reference the same chamber, and multiple valves can connect to one another seamlessly.

It won’t be perfect—nothing ever is. And I’m sure Ange pays a significant cost to maintain the complexity of his physics-based simulation. But I’m confident that my system will be good enough to make a strong impression once again.
